Ask five university career services how a career changer should format a CV and you get a
genuine split. Oxford, JMU and GWU all point changers towards a skills-first — "functional" —
layout that leads with transferable abilities instead of job titles. The University of Alabama's
career centre warns, in plain words: "Not all employers like functional resumes because they
prefer to see the details of accomplishments with each position held."
**The format built for career changers is the one some employers trust least — and the
institutions advising you know it.**

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents is treating "transferable skills" as permission to hide your
work history. Changers hear "lead with skills", strip the dates and employers off the page, and
end up with a CV that reads as evasive to the very people it was meant to persuade.

Two different things are being conflated:

- **Transferable-skills content** — describing what you did in your old field in terms the new
  field recognises. Every source quoted below endorses this.
- **The functional format** — reorganising the whole CV under skills headings, pushing the
  job-by-job history down or out. This is the contested part.

## What "transferable skills" actually means — per the sources

Oxford describes the skills-based CV like this:

> "This type of CV is used to highlight the transferability of your skills, and so is useful if
> you are applying to a role without direct experience."

> "In a skills-based CV, the information is arranged to highlight relevant employability skills,
> with details presented under different skills categories."

JMU makes the career-change link explicit:

> "Since functional resumes emphasize your skills, they can be useful for when you want to enter
> into a different career field or illustrate your transferable skills."

GWU lists the functional format as suiting you if "You are changing careers", "You want to
highlight your transferable skills", or "You have diverse experiences that do not add up to a
clear cut career path".

So the framing itself is not controversial: show the new employer that what you did before maps
onto what they need. The fight is over how the page is organised.

## The format debate, in full

**For the functional format** — JMU and GWU above, plus Alabama's own qualified endorsement:

> "This format may work well for some career changers with experience in areas outside the new
> profession." — University of Alabama

> "For example, a veteran with military experience may choose a functional resume as a way of
> showing how experiences and skills translate to civilian jobs." — University of Alabama

**Against, or cautious:**

> "Not all employers like functional resumes because they prefer to see the details of
> accomplishments with each position held." — University of Alabama

> Many employers "are not as familiar with this format." — Stanford Career Education, which
> notes the chronological "format is most familiar to employers and most commonly used by
> Stanford students."

> "We generally only recommend using this style if you have great experience, as a considerable
> amount of evidence is required." — Oxford, on its own skills-based CV

And every source agrees on the baseline. Oxford: "The traditional – or 'reverse chronological' –
CV is the most commonly used format." JMU, on chronological: "It is the most common resume
format and the one most employers prefer." GWU: chronological is "by far the most commonly used
resume type."

**Read that carefully: the same institutions recommending the functional format for changers
also state that employers prefer the chronological one.** Nobody resolves that tension for you,
so we won't pretend to either.

## The middle path most sources gesture at

JMU defines a third option:

> "A combination resume includes aspects of both chronological and functional formats.
> Experiences and skills are emphasized equally."

That is as far as the sourced material goes. The rest is coaching, and we label it as such: a
common practical compromise is to keep the reverse-chronological employment history intact —
dates, employers, titles — and add a short transferable-skills summary at the top that does the
translation work. You get the skills-first framing the pro-functional sources want, without
triggering the distrust Alabama and Stanford describe. No source above prescribes this exact
layout; it is simply the shape that honours both sides of the quoted disagreement.

## Worked example (coaching, not a sourced fact)

The same retail-management experience, rewritten to face a project-coordination job — nothing
invented, only re-described:

**Before (facing retail):**
- Managed a team of 8 sales assistants and ran the weekly rota

**After (facing project coordination):**
- Coordinated an 8-person team's schedules and workloads, reallocating cover at short notice
  to keep the store fully staffed through peak trading

Why each move works: "managed a team" becomes "coordinated schedules and workloads" — the
project-coordination vocabulary for the identical activity. "Ran the weekly rota" becomes
"reallocating cover at short notice" — resource planning under constraint, which is the job
being applied for. The employer, the dates and the retail context stay on the page. The
translation happens inside the bullet, not by deleting the history around it.

## What nobody has measured

Here is the honest limit: **no study in our fact base measures which format actually wins more
interviews for career changers.** The disagreement between career services — JMU and GWU
recommending functional for changers, Alabama and Stanford warning that employers distrust or
don't recognise it, Oxford recommending its skills-based CV while restricting it to people with
"great experience" — is the current state of knowledge. Anyone telling you one format is
proven to outperform the other for changers is asserting something these institutions, between
them, do not. Check what your target employer or sector expects before choosing.

## What actually costs you

- Using skills headings to hide the history — readers who "prefer to see the details of
  accomplishments with each position held" will notice the absence.
- Listing transferable skills without evidence — Oxford's warning that "a considerable amount
  of evidence is required" applies to any skills claim, in any format.
- Keeping your old field's vocabulary — a bullet that still speaks retail to a project-management
  reader has transferred nothing.
- Inventing experience during the rewrite — re-describe what happened; never upgrade it.
- Assuming one format is universally safe — the sources themselves disagree; sectors and
  countries differ, so check with the target employer.
