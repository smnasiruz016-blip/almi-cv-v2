MIT's career service tells first-time applicants plainly that "experience comes in many forms" —
and then lists class projects, competitions and personal projects as things to include. The
blank-page panic comes from defining experience as salaried employment, and that is a definition
no career service uses. **The institutions that see the most first CVs have already widened the
definition for you — the job is to write down what you did, not to have done something else.**

## The failure

The specific mistake this page prevents: staring at an empty Experience section, deciding you
have nothing to put in it, and either sending a half-empty page or padding it with invented
titles and inflated language. Both fail differently. The half-empty page hides real evidence you
already have. The padded page collides with the honesty baseline governments publish for
jobseekers — Canada's Job Bank puts it bluntly:

> "Lying on your resume is never a good idea. You don't want to overstate your skills or results
> as it will mislead the employer."

## What institutions say counts

This is one of the rare CV questions where the sources do not contradict each other. Every
institution in our fact base converges on the same answer.

MIT Career Advising & Professional Development:

> "experience comes in many forms. In addition to jobs, internships, UROPS or leadership roles,
> consider including class projects, competitions or even personal projects."

Cornell Career Services, in a post titled exactly for this problem — "No Work Experience?
Here's What to Highlight Instead" — defines experience to include:

> "class projects, relevant coursework, volunteer work, campus involvement, side gigs, part-time
> jobs, and even personal learning or independent projects."

Oxford's Careers Service says the same from the UK side: "Even if you don't have much paid work
experience, you can include voluntary work or contributions you have made to clubs or societies."
Stanford's resume guide adds that freshmen and sophomores can include high school experiences,
and that relevant high school material "may be appropriate for upper classmen" too. The
University of Kentucky's career centre is happy for "large course projects" to go straight into
the experience section.

And it is not only universities coaching their own students. USAJOBS — the US federal
government's own hiring site — says:

> "Include relevant volunteer work and roles in community organizations that demonstrate how you
> qualify for the job."

**If a federal hiring system accepts volunteer work as qualifying experience, your first CV can
too.** The material you think doesn't count — the group project, the society role, the weekend
shifts, the thing you built for yourself — is exactly what these sources tell you to write down.

## What goes first

Here we have to be honest about what the sources actually say. **No institution in our fact base
states "put Education first" as a quotable rule.** What Harvard's Mignone Center does state is
the principle behind the convention:

> "List headings (such as Experience) in order of importance"

and, within each heading, "list information in reverse chronological order (most recent first)".

The education-first layout you see in nearly every student CV template is coaching derived from
that principle: for a first CV, your degree, modules and projects are usually the strongest
evidence you have, so "order of importance" tends to put Education at the top. Treat that as the
common convention it is, not a rule — and if the strongest thing on your page is a year of
part-time work or a substantial project, the same Harvard principle argues for leading with that
instead. Check what the target employer asks for; some application systems impose their own
order anyway.

## Worked example: one group project, two bullets

Raw material — labelled as a worked example, not a quote from any source. A student's honest
description of a university group project:

*"I was in a group of four for our second-year software module. We built a web app for booking
study rooms. I mostly did the booking logic and helped test it."*

Turned into two CV bullets:

- **Built the booking and availability logic for a room-reservation web app, in a team of four,
  delivered over a 10-week module.**
- **Wrote and ran the test cases for the booking flow, fixing the defects found before the final
  demo.**

Per-line reasoning:

- *"Built the booking and availability logic"* — leads with a verb and the specific component
  owned, not "was involved in". "In a team of four" and "10-week module" are the honest numbers
  already in the story; nothing is invented, the vague "mostly did" becomes a concrete claim.
- *"Wrote and ran the test cases… fixing the defects found"* — "helped test it" is the kind of
  line that evaporates on a CV. Naming what the testing produced (defects found and fixed before
  the demo) turns a filler verb into evidence. This is the same move our quantified-bullets page
  teaches: make small things concrete rather than making them sound big.

Note what the rewrite does *not* do: no "Software Engineer" title, no "led", no made-up user
numbers. The scale stays true; only the specificity changes.

## What not to pad with

Labelled coaching, consistent with the sources above:

- **No invented job titles.** "Founder" for a hobby, "Consultant" for helping a relative —
  titles imply an employer relationship that didn't exist, and that is the overstating Canada's
  Job Bank warns against.
- **No inflated language.** "Spearheaded", "orchestrated" and "drove" on a group coursework
  bullet read as compensation. The worked example above got stronger by getting more specific,
  not more grand.
- **No filler entries.** A one-day workshop listed as experience dilutes the real entries next
  to it. If it belongs anywhere, it belongs under skills or coursework.

## What the sources don't settle

- No institution we could verify publishes the sentence "students should place Education first".
  The convention is reflected in Harvard, Stanford and Yale sample templates, but as an implied
  practice, not a quoted rule — which is why we presented it as coaching above.
- UK National Careers Service guidance for young people writing a first CV could not be
  retrieved (the pages block automated access), so the UK government position specifically on
  no-experience CVs is unquoted here. Oxford stands in for the UK academic side.
- None of these sources says how much a first CV's project section is *weighted* by any given
  employer. Nobody publishes that, and we won't pretend to know it.

## What actually costs you

- Leaving the page half empty because the material didn't come with a payslip.
- Inventing a title or inflating a verb — the one move the government sources explicitly warn
  against.
- Vague bullets ("helped with", "was involved in") where a specific, honest one was available.
- Copying an education-first template without asking whether it's your strongest section — the
  actual principle is order of importance.
