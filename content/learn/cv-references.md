Ask the UK government whether your CV needs a references section and the answer is yes — it lists
references as a standard CV section alongside your contact details and work history. Ask Harvard or
the Government of Canada and the answer is the opposite: leave references off entirely. **These are
not a careful source and a careless one — they are official, current guidance from both sides of
the Atlantic, and they flatly disagree.** Whichever advice you copy, it is wrong somewhere.

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents is treating one country's references advice as universal. It happens
in both directions. A UK applicant reads a North American guide, strips the references section, and
sends a CV into a market whose own national careers service lists references as an expected section.
A candidate applying into Canada keeps the referee block a UK template gave them — and spends two or
three lines of a two-page limit on content the Government of Canada's own guidance says not to
include at all.

Neither version of the document is broken. Each is simply built for the other market.

## What the sources actually say

**The UK position — include a references section.** The UK National Careers Service, describing
what a CV should contain:

> "Your CV should include a section for your contact details, an introduction, your education
> history, your work history and references."
> — [UK National Careers Service](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections)

References sit in that list as a standard section, on the same footing as your work history.

**The North American position — leave references off.** Harvard's Mignone Center for Career
Success lists, among the things you should *not* do on a resume:

> "List references"
> — [Harvard Mignone Center](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/)

Canada's Job Bank — the Government of Canada's employment service — is just as blunt:

> "Do not include references"
> — [Canada Job Bank](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/findajob/resources/write-good-resume)

| Source | Type | Position on references |
|---|---|---|
| UK National Careers Service | UK government | A CV "should include" a references section |
| Harvard Mignone Center | US university career service | Don't: "List references" |
| Canada Job Bank | Canadian government | "Do not include references" |

**This is a genuine divergence in official guidance, not a difference of tone.** It also tracks the
wider pattern: the same national sources split the same way on photos, personal data and page count
— the same document norms simply differ by market. Our country-specific convention pages carry the
per-country detail; this page's job is the principle.

## Worked example: the same section, two markets

Here is a typical references block as UK templates render it:

```
References
Available on request.
```

or, fuller:

```
References
J. Whitfield, Operations Manager, [company] — [email] — [phone]
```

Read it against each market's guidance:

- **Applying in the UK:** the National Careers Service lists references among the sections a CV
  should include — so a references section here is following the official national guidance, not
  padding.
- **Applying in Canada:** the Job Bank's instruction is "Do not include references" — every line of
  this block is content the official guidance says to cut, spent inside the same source's two-page
  limit.
- **Applying in the US:** Harvard's don'ts include "List references" — the fuller version above,
  with a named contact and their details, is exactly what that guidance rules out.

Same three lines, three different verdicts — and none of the verdicts is about the quality of the
writing. **The market, not the section, decides.**

## What the sources don't settle

Honesty about the edges of the fact base:

- **"References available on request."** None of the official sources quoted above takes a position
  on this phrase. The UK guidance asks for a references section without prescribing its wording; the
  North American sources rule the whole section out, which makes the phrase moot in those markets.
  We found no official line endorsing or banning it, so we won't invent one.
- **Who to choose as a referee.** The sources here establish *whether* a references section belongs
  on the page, not *who* should be in it. We have no verified official guidance on referee selection
  to pass on, so this page doesn't offer any.
- **The rest of the world.** The clean split above covers three countries. Don't extrapolate it —
  check the convention page for your target country, and above all check the job posting itself.
  A posting that explicitly asks for referees overrides the general convention wherever you are;
  these are conventions, not rules, and the employer's stated requirements always win. When in
  doubt, check with the target employer.

## What actually costs you

- **Copying one market's advice into another.** Both directions fail: no references section where
  the UK's own careers service expects one, or a referee block in a Canadian application whose
  official guidance says "Do not include references."
- **Spending scarce space on it in a capped market.** Canada's Job Bank both caps the resume at two
  pages and excludes references — the section costs you lines and follows no local guidance.
- **Ignoring the posting.** If the advert asks for referees, the convention argument is over —
  supply what the employer asked for, in the format they asked for it.
- **Assuming the split generalises.** Three countries' official positions are quoted here; other
  markets have their own conventions, covered on their own pages.
