Most countries make you infer their CV conventions from templates and folklore. The UK is the
market where the official sources actually spell them out — the government's National Careers
Service lists the sections a CV "should include", and both it and Oxford's Careers Service name,
item by item, the personal details to leave off. **And one of those official instructions puts
British guidance in direct contradiction with North America's.**

## The failure

The failure this page prevents is importing someone else's conventions into a British application —
usually one of two ways.

First: treating a UK CV like a continental European one, with a photo, a date of birth, a
nationality line and a marital status field. British official guidance names most of that as
material to remove.

Second: treating it like a US resume and silently deleting the references section — when the UK's
own government careers service lists references as part of what a CV should include.

## What to leave off — quoted

The National Careers Service is blunt about personal data:

> "You should not include your age, your date of birth, whether you're married or your
> nationality."
> — UK National Careers Service, [CV sections](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections)

Oxford's Careers Service goes further, with a longer exclusion list:

> "You don't need to include the following in a CV: The words 'Curriculum Vitae' or 'CV'… Date of
> birth and/or age… Marital status, disability, children, partner, sexual preferences, sex, racial
> background, religion… Home address."
> — [Oxford University Careers Service](https://www.careers.ox.ac.uk/cvs)

Two honest footnotes. Neither captured UK quote actually says the word "photo" — the explicit
anti-photo lines in official guidance come from North America (Canada's Job Bank: a photo "is not
the norm in Canada. It can actually lower your chances"; Harvard lists "Include a picture" among
its don'ts). The UK exclusion lists are about data fields, and UK templates are conventionally
photo-free, but if you want a source that names photos, it sits across the Atlantic — see the
personal-data page. And on addresses, the two UK sources genuinely differ in scope: Oxford says
you don't need a home address, while the NCS still asks for "a section for your contact details"
without ruling the address in or out. That is a soft disagreement, not a contradiction — both
agree contact details belong; Oxford narrows what counts.

## What goes on — including the line that splits the Atlantic

Here is the NCS on structure, in full:

> "Your CV should include a section for your contact details, an introduction, your education
> history, your work history and references."
> — UK National Careers Service

Read that last word again. **References — the section US and Canadian official guidance tells you
to cut — is listed by the UK government as a standard part of the CV.** Harvard's don'ts include
"List references"; Canada's Job Bank says flatly "Do not include references". This is a genuine
documented contradiction between official sources, and we report it rather than resolve it: which
advice applies depends on which side of the Atlantic the employer sits. The full split, both sides
quoted, is on the references page.

Section order is a convention, not a law, and the NCS says so itself:

> "If you have a lot of work experience, you might want to change the order and show off your work
> history and experience first."

Oxford's traditional ordering:

> "The sections of the traditional CV will normally be as follows: Personal information…
> Education… Experience – the core of your CV… Additional skills… Interests."

with education "normally at the top (especially for recent graduates entering the jobs market for
the first or second time)".

## A UK header, torn down

A header written to continental habits, against the quotes above, line by line:

```
CURRICULUM VITAE                    ← Oxford: you don't need "the words 'Curriculum Vitae' or 'CV'"
[photo]                             ← no captured UK quote bans it; no UK source asks for it either
DOB: 14/03/1994 · British · Married ← NCS: not age, not date of birth, not marital status, not nationality
42 Elm Grove, Sheffield S10 2XX     ← Oxford: home address on its don't-need list
```

What survives is a name, a phone number, an email — the "section for your contact details" the NCS
asks for — and then the CV proper: introduction, education, work history, and (in the UK framing)
references. Every deleted line is a line that carried legal-risk data instead of evidence you can
do the job.

## Length — what is actually verifiable

Oxford is on the record:

> "Keep it to one or two full pages (academic CVs may be longer)—check if there is specified page
> limit in the application guidance."

The National Careers Service is not. You have likely heard "two sides of A4" attributed to the
NCS — **the current NCS CV page gives no page-count recommendation at all**; that attribution
appears only on third-party sites. So the honest statement is: the verifiable UK official position
is Oxford's one-to-two full pages, with an instruction to check the application's own limit, and
the government service is simply silent on length.

## "CV" means the normal document here

In Britain, CV is not the academic long-form document it is in US usage — it is the standard
application document, and Oxford says the two words name the same thing:

> "A curriculum vitae (or résumé in the US) is a concise summary of your skills, achievements, and
> interests inside and outside of your academic work."

The NCS matches: "A CV is a summary of your skills, achievements and experience that you use to
apply for jobs." If you are reading US advice about multi-page academic CVs, that is a different
document — see the United States page for that side of the split.

## What the official pages don't cover

Being honest about the gaps in the UK sources: neither captured page addresses photos by name, the
NCS gives no length figure, and neither page discusses file formats, ATS parsing, or how UK
recruiters actually read what arrives — none of that is in the official guidance, so none of it is
asserted here. And all of the above is convention, not statute: common in the UK, advised by
British sources — **if the job posting or the employer's own guidance says otherwise, the posting
wins. Check it before you send.**

## What actually costs you

- A date of birth, nationality or marital status line — the NCS says not to include them.
- A header that says "Curriculum Vitae" — Oxford lists the words themselves as unnecessary.
- Cutting references because a US guide said so, without checking the UK framing — the NCS lists
  them as a standard section.
- Citing "two sides of A4" as official — it is not on the current NCS page; Oxford's "one or two
  full pages" is the quotable position.
- Ignoring the posting: Oxford itself says to check for a specified page limit in the application
  guidance.
