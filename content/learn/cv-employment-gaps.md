The UK government's National Careers Service tells jobseekers, in writing: "Having a gap in
your CV is not a problem." Harvard Business School and Accenture, in their Hidden Workers
research, documented employers whose screening software automatically rejects any work history
with a gap of more than six months — "based on that consideration alone". **Both statements
are real, both are current, and an honest page about employment gaps has to hold both at
once.**

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents is picking one of those two stories and betting your CV on it.

Believe only the reassurance, and you send an unexplained two-year hole into systems that —
at some employers — reject it before a human looks. Believe only the filtering research, and
you start hiding dates, stretching end dates, or switching to a format designed to bury the
gap — and a hole discovered at interview, or a CV in a format many employers distrust, can
cost you more than the gap ever would.

The honest position: **you cannot know in advance which kind of employer you are sending to,
so present the gap in a way that survives both.**

## What the reassurance side actually says

The National Careers Service's advice is not naive — read past the headline sentence and it
is about preparation, not denial:

> "Having a gap in your CV is not a problem, but you might be asked about it."

> "The important thing is to be open and honest, and that you're ready to explain what you
> were doing during any gaps."

> "Concentrate on any transferable skills you gained during your gap in employment and what
> you have learned from the experience."

It even blesses the small stuff — "you may have done some volunteering, or completed a
short or online course"; "Even if you do not think it's relevant to the job, it shows
you're willing to learn."

Oxford's Careers Service lands in the same place:

> "We recommend that you explain any significant time gaps in your CV. There is no right or
> wrong way of presenting your personal circumstances."

Oxford's examples of legitimate gaps: "You may have been travelling, working on an
independent project (e.g. writing), been ill, or caring for others."

So the reassurance side is really an instruction: gaps happen, explain them, be honest.

## What the filtering side actually says

The Hidden Workers report (Fuller, Raman et al., Harvard Business School and Accenture, 2021)
surveyed employers about how their recruitment management systems and ATS are configured. Its
finding on gaps is blunt:

> "If an applicant's work history has a gap of more than six months, the resume is
> automatically screened out by their RMS or ATS, based on that consideration alone."

> "A recruiter will never see that candidate's application, even though it might fill all of
> the employer's requirements."

How widespread? The report says "Almost half the companies surveyed weeded out resumes that
present such a 'work gap.'" Its figures put that at 48% of employers filtering middle-skills
candidates on gaps of more than six months, within a landscape where "more than 90% of
employers used their RMS to initially filter or rank potential middle-skills (94%) and
high-skills (92%) candidates."

The report is specific about who gets caught: candidates "who left the workforce for a
period of more than six months for reasons such as a difficult pregnancy, the illness of a
spouse or dependent, personal, physical, or mental health needs, or relocation due to a new
posting of a military spouse, are eliminated from consideration."

**Note what this is and is not.** It is a survey of employer configuration, published in
2021, largely about US hiring. It is not evidence that most CVs with gaps are auto-rejected
everywhere — and it directly contradicts the idea that gaps never matter to software.

## The format question — a documented disagreement

One institutional answer to gaps is to change CV format. George Washington University's
GSEHD career service lists, as a reason to choose a functional (skills-first) resume:
"You want to minimize gaps in employment" — and says chronological suits you if "You have no
significant gaps in employment."

Other sources push the other way. James Madison University notes of the chronological
format that "It may show gaps in your career path" — but also that "It is the most common
resume format and the one most employers prefer." And other university sources warn that
functional CVs themselves raise eyebrows: not all employers like them, and many are less
familiar with the format (the career-change page covers this debate in full).

**We report that contradiction rather than resolve it.** One camp says a functional format
legitimately de-emphasises gaps; the other says employers prefer chronological and are wary
of formats that hide the work history. If you are considering a functional CV mainly to bury
a gap, know that the format choice itself is contested.

## What to do with your own gap — coaching, not sourced fact

What follows is our practical read of the sources above, labelled as coaching.

**Dates you cannot change; framing you can.** The gap exists. The choice you control is
whether the reader meets it with an explanation attached or discovers it unexplained.

**A one-line explanation beats a hidden gap discovered later.** A single dated line in the
work history — worked example:

Before: *ABC Ltd, Marketing Executive, 2019–2021* … *XYZ plc, Marketing Manager, 2023–present*
— a silent two-year hole the reader fills with their own worst guess.

After, one line added between them: *2021–2023 — Career break: full-time carer for a family
member; completed CIM digital marketing certificate (online).*

That line does three things the sources ask for: it is honest (NCS: "be open and honest"),
it names the circumstance without apology (Oxford: "no right or wrong way of presenting your
personal circumstances"), and it surfaces something learned (NCS: courses and volunteering
"show you're willing to learn"). It also keeps the CV chronological — the format employers
are said to prefer — instead of restructuring the whole document around one gap.

**Prepare the spoken version too.** NCS's core point is that you "might be asked about it".
Two or three sentences, factual and forward-looking, cover it.

## What is not known

Be clear about the limits of the evidence — the split itself is the finding.

- **No data exists on the ratio.** Nobody has published how many employers configure gap
  filters versus how many follow the "not a problem" line. The Hidden Workers survey gives
  "almost half" for its 2021 sample; the NCS speaks for official UK guidance. Which one your
  next application hits is unknowable from the outside.
- The Hidden Workers figures date from 2021 and skew US; we have no verified equivalent
  measurement for UK employers' ATS configurations.
- The NCS's main CV-writing guidance could not be checked this research cycle; only its
  gaps page was retrievable.

## What actually costs you

- An unexplained gap in a system configured to filter it — rejected unseen.
- A hidden or fudged gap discovered at interview or referencing — an honesty problem now,
  which is worse than a gap.
- Restructuring your whole CV into a contested format to bury one gap, when a one-line
  explanation would have done.
- Assuming either story — "gaps never matter" or "gaps always kill you" — is universal.

## Build it

Write the gap line once, honestly, and keep the rest of the document parseable and
chronological — then check anything unusual with the target employer, because conventions
differ and configurations are invisible.
