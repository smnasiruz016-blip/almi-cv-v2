Every CV article you have ever read leans on the same four numbers. 75% of CVs rejected by
software. Six seconds per CV. 99% of the Fortune 500. One page, always. We chased each one
back to its origin — **one was never a study at all, one is a small vendor test misquoted
for a decade, and one is contradicted by the only controlled study anyone has run.**

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents: rebuilding your CV around a number nobody ever measured.
People keyword-stuff, buy "ATS-beating" rewrites, and amputate a second page of genuinely
relevant experience — all to satisfy statistics that dissolve the moment you ask who counted.

Here is each famous number, traced.

## Case 1: "75% of CVs are rejected by ATS before a human sees them"

**Verdict: no study has ever been produced — and the claim is now actively contradicted.**

Recruiter and author Jan Tegze traced the claim and hit three dead ends. A 2014 Forbes
contributor piece asserting *"Studies have shown that up to 75% of qualified applicants are
rejected by ATS programs because they can't be read"* — written, Tegze notes, by "the founder
of a resume service" and "more of an advertisement than an article." A 2018 CIO.com article
with, in his words, "no relevant data or study" cited. And the now-defunct resume-optimisation
vendor Preptel:

> "several resume-writing services and many articles say their sources are these articles I
> mentioned or the search firm Preptel (which is now out of business), but what a 'surprise' —
> they were also offering resume-writing services."

The likeliest seed is a 2013 ERE.net illustration of a hiring funnel — "75 of those 100
resumes will be screened out by either the ATS or a recruiter" — which describes total
human-plus-software screening, not robots auto-rejecting.

Then someone finally asked recruiters. In late 2025, Enhancv interviewed 25 US recruiters
across tech, healthcare, finance, manufacturing and more, at companies from under 100 to
50,000+ employees:

> "23 of 25 (92%) said rejections are manual or triggered only by eligibility filters —
> never by formatting or missing keywords."

Only 8% had configured auto-rejection on content matching; 84% use knockout questions —
work authorisation, certifications, location. Asked where the myth comes from, "68% pointed
to job seekers who saw it on LinkedIn or TikTok, and 20% blamed career coaches and resume
blogs recycling outdated advice." IT Brief UK and HR.com both covered it — HR.com's headline:
"ATS Rejection Myth Debunked: 92% of Recruiters Confirm Applicant Tracking Systems Do NOT
Automatically Reject Resumes."

Small sample, yes — 25 recruiters. But it is 25 more data points than the 75% ever had.

## Case 2: "Recruiters spend six seconds on your CV"

**Verdict: a real source exists — a small vendor test, misquoted for over a decade.**

The origin is TheLadders, a job board, in a 2012 eye-tracking whitepaper: roughly 30
professional recruiters, tracked over ten weeks. The press release claimed recruiters "spend
just six seconds reviewing a resume before deciding if the candidate is a good fit." A 2018
update revised the figure — "the average initial screen clocking in at just 7.4 seconds in
2018" — with recruiters "who were not aware they were being timed" skimming a stack to
identify potential candidates.

Note what was measured: **the initial fit/no-fit skim, not total reading time.** CVs that
pass the skim get read properly. Neither study was peer-reviewed; both were marketing
research by a job board; the 2018 PDF does not even state its sample size. The honest
version is: "In Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study, the initial screen averaged 7.4 seconds —
a first skim, not a full read." The useful lesson survives the caveats: the 2012 study found
almost 80% of review time went to six data points — name, current and previous titles and
companies, dates, education. Make those findable.

## Case 3: "99% of the Fortune 500 use an ATS"

**Verdict: real, repeatable measurement — by a vendor, and the number moves.**

Jobscan, an ATS-optimisation vendor, measures this annually: *"We reverse-engineered the
career pages for each company on the list"* — detecting which ATS serves each application
page, without contacting companies. The figure was 98.2% in 2018, 98.8% in 2019, and 97.4%
(487 of 500) in the current report. Roughly accurate, then — but note the sleight of hand
it usually props up: **using an ATS proves nothing about auto-rejection.** An ATS is a
workflow database. See Case 1.

## Case 4: "Your CV must always be one page"

**Verdict: no supporting study exists — and the only controlled study points the other way.**

The one-page rule is print-era career-office convention. We found no empirical study behind
it. The one controlled study on record is ResumeGo's 2018 simulated hiring experiment: 482
recruitment professionals screened 7,712 CVs, each one-pager paired with a two-page
counterpart of equivalent experience. Verbatim:

> "recruiters were 2.3 times as likely to prefer two-page resumes over one-page resumes"

Two-pagers scored 8.6 on average against 7.1. The preference held at every level — 1.4x for
entry-level, 2.6x mid-level, 2.9x managerial. Caveats apply: vendor research, simulated
hiring, not peer-reviewed. But a rule with zero studies loses to a finding with one.
Length should follow experience, not a rule.

## The derivative stats

Three more circulate as garnish, and none survives tracing:

| Claim | What tracing found |
|---|---|
| "250 CVs per corporate opening" | Glassdoor listicle, no source cited; echoes a c.2011–13 ERE.net funnel sketch. Enhancv's 2025 interviews put entry-level roles at 400–600 applicants — the number is stale either way |
| "76% binned for an unprofessional email address" | No survey has ever been produced; sites cite each other in a circle |
| "Only 2% of applicants get interviews" | Arithmetic on the unsourced 250 figure — folklore built on folklore |

## What the research actually shows

There is real, rigorous CV research — it just tells a less convenient story. Bertrand and
Mullainathan (*American Economic Review*, 2004) sent nearly 5,000 fictitious CVs, randomly
assigned "African-American- or White-sounding names," to over 1,300 job ads:

> "White names receive 50 percent more callbacks for interviews."

And Harvard Business School's "Hidden Workers" report (2021; 8,000+ workers, 2,250+
executives surveyed) is the legitimate version of the "software screens people out" story:
88% of employers agree qualified high-skills candidates "are vetted out of the process
because they do not match the exact criteria established by the job description" — 94% for
middle-skills roles. Note what that describes: **human-configured filter criteria, not a
robot rejecting your font.** The real screening problem is real — it just is not the 75%.

## What actually costs you

- Keyword-stuffing to beat a rejection robot that 92% of surveyed recruiters say does not exist.
- Cutting a relevant second page to obey a rule with no study behind it.
- Burying name, titles, companies and dates — the six things the eye-tracking data says get
  read first.
- Believing any statistic before asking three questions: **who measured it, how, and does the
  claim match what was actually measured?** Every debunking on this page is one of those
  three questions.
