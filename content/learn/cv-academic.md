Almost every rule on this site points one way: shorter, tighter, tailored to a single
job. The academic CV is the one document where that advice reverses. MIT's own guidance
puts it at two to seven pages, it grows over a career rather than being trimmed, and it
leads with material a job application plays down — publications, conferences, grants.

**The academic CV is not a long resume. It is a different document with a different
audience, and the two do not convert without rebuilding.**

## The failure

The failure runs in both directions, and each direction undersells you.

Direction one: you apply for a PhD, a fellowship or a lectureship with the one-page
document this site helped you compress. A hiring committee wants your research record —
and a page built to job-market rules has squeezed exactly that record out. Using a job
CV for an academic application undersells you as much as the reverse oversells.

Direction two: you leave academia and send your five-page CV to an employer. Harvard's
guidance for PhD students moving to industry is blunt about what that market expects:

> "A resume is typically shorter, 1-2 pages at most, and will dedicate more space to
> your experience while focusing less on academic awards, conference presentations,
> and publications."
> — Harvard Mignone Center for Career Success

Neither document is wrong. Each is wrong for the other door.

## What the academic CV is — in the institutions' own words

In US usage, "CV" names the academic document specifically. MIT's Career Advising &
Professional Development office defines its territory:

> "In the United States, curricula vitae (CVs) are used to apply for teaching or
> research opportunities, fellowships, further academic training, grants, contract
> funding proposals, tenure, promotion to professor positions, and membership in a
> professional society or organization."
> — MIT CAPD

Note what that list has in common: every use is a claim about your scholarly record,
judged by people who read scholarly records for a living. That is why the document is
allowed — required — to be complete rather than concise.

Be aware the word itself branches by region. Oxford's careers service treats CV and
résumé as one document with two names — "A curriculum vitae (or résumé in the US) is a
concise summary of your skills, achievements, and interests inside and outside of your
academic work" — while MIT treats them as separate documents for separate markets. MIT
flags the trap directly: "In some international settings, the term 'CV' is used for all
opportunities – however, you should tailor your document based on position type." When
a posting says "CV", check which sense it means before you pick a length.

## The length quotes

This is the number that reverses everything. MIT, on the academic CV:

> "The length is typically 2 to 4 pages for a younger professional, 4 to 7 pages for a
> person with more experience."
> — MIT CAPD

Read that against MIT's own resume advice — "Stick to one page, unless you have
extensive experience or an advanced degree" — and against Oxford's aside that "academic
CVs may be longer" than the one-to-two-page norm. **The same institutions that police a
one-page resume expect a multi-page academic CV, and expect it to lengthen as your
record does.** A two-page academic CV at four pages of career is not admirable
restraint; it reads as a thin record.

## What it carries

The institutional quotes above name the raw material. Harvard's PhD guidance identifies
publications, conference presentations and academic awards as the content a resume cuts
— which is precisely the content an academic CV keeps. MIT's list of uses — teaching,
research, fellowships, grants — maps to the record the document must evidence.

As coaching, not sourced rule: academics reading a CV expect dedicated sections for
publications, teaching and research, and expect you to lead with whichever your target
role weighs most — research first for a research post, teaching first for a
teaching-focused one. That ordering advice is ours; the institutions quoted here do not
prescribe a section order for academic CVs.

## The conversion, worked

Take one real line from a PhD student's academic CV:

> Chen, L., **You, A.**, et al. (2025). [Paper title]. *Journal of X*, 14(2).
> Presented at [Conference], 2025.

On the academic CV this line is doing its job: full citation, authorship position
visible, the conference presentation logged. It stays.

Converting to an industry resume, Harvard's guidance says the receiving document
"will dedicate more space to your experience while focusing less on academic awards,
conference presentations, and publications." So the citation does not move across —
the work behind it does, rewritten as experience:

> Led data analysis for a 3-person research project; findings published in a
> peer-reviewed journal.

What changed, line by line: the citation format is gone because an employer will not
parse one; the journal's name is gone because it signals nothing outside the field;
what appears instead is the activity (led analysis), the scale (3-person project) and
the externally checkable outcome (peer-reviewed publication). Running the conversion
the other way, the same logic reverses: an industry bullet's business outcome matters
less to a committee than the methods, outputs and funding it can file under a section.

## What is not settled

Honesty about the edges of this page. The quotes here come from US and UK institutions
— MIT, Harvard, Oxford. Academic CV conventions differ by field and by country: what a
US committee, a UK panel and a continental European one expect can diverge on length,
personal details and section names, and our sources do not cover every case. No source
in our base prescribes a universal academic section order, and we have no institutional
quote on field-specific conventions. **If your target department or funder publishes
its own CV guidance, that guidance outranks anything on this page.**

## What actually costs you

- Sending a one-page job document to a committee that expected your full record.
- Sending a 5-page publication list to an employer Harvard says wants 1–2 pages.
- Trimming an academic CV to look "tidy" — thinness reads as a thin record.
- Assuming "CV" means the same thing in every country. It does not; check the sense.
- Ignoring a department's own CV guidance in favour of general advice — including ours.
