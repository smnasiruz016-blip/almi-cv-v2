Three institutions that share almost nothing — the UK's National Careers Service, Oxford's
Careers Service, Harvard's Mignone Center — publish cover letter guidance independently, and
all three land on the same shape. The National Careers Service calls it "a short letter,
usually 3 to 5 paragraphs long"; Harvard says "Keep it to one page. Three to four short
paragraphs"; Oxford says "brevity adds power and aim for no longer than 1 side of A4 in
length". **When sources this different converge this exactly, the shape is settled — what is
not settled, judging by real letters, is what the paragraphs are for.**

## The failure

The wasted paragraph is nearly always the first one. It re-lists the CV: "I am a motivated
professional with four years of experience in warehouse operations and a proven track record
of team leadership." The reader is holding the CV. They can see the four years.

Harvard's 2026 guidance names this precisely:

> "A cover letter isn't a summary of your resume." … "Its real job is to answer two questions
> your resume can't: Why this role? and Why you?"

The National Careers Service assigns the opening a different job entirely — "Introduce
yourself and explain how you found the advertised job" — and Oxford asks the middle to
"Explain why you are interested in the job and the organisation" and "Explain why you are
well-suited to the position". **None of the three institutions asks you to summarise the CV
anywhere in the letter.** The letter that repeats the CV fails all three briefs at once.

## One letter, torn down

Here is a full letter for one invented, deliberately specific situation: a warehouse shift
lead with four years' experience applying for a team leader opening at Ferrow Logistics, a
fictional firm launching a chilled-grocery depot in Doncaster. This is an original AlmiCV
example — the applicant, the firm and the posting are all invented.

> Dear Ms Achebe,
>
> I'm applying for the Team Leader role at Ferrow Logistics' new Doncaster depot, advertised
> on your careers page last week. For the past four years I've been a shift lead at a
> chilled-food distribution warehouse in Rotherham, running a picking team of eleven on the
> 6am–2pm rotation — and the move into temperature-controlled grocery described in your
> posting is the operation I know from the inside.
>
> Chilled distribution punishes slow handovers: stock that waits too long at the dock is
> stock you write off. I rebuilt our shift-change routine around a fifteen-minute overlap
> and a single shared exceptions sheet, and I've trained every new picker on that routine
> since 2023. Your posting asks for someone who can "hold pick accuracy through peak" —
> that is the specific problem my team and I solve every December.
>
> I'd welcome the chance to talk about how the Doncaster site will handle its first winter
> peak. My CV has the detail of my roles and training; thank you for considering my
> application, and I look forward to hearing from you.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Daniel Mears

Now hold each paragraph against the institutional guidance.

**Paragraph one** does the National Careers Service's assigned job — introduces the
applicant and "explain[s] how you found the advertised job" — and simultaneously answers
Harvard's "Why this role?". The four years appear, but attached to a claim the CV cannot
make: that his niche and the employer's new venture are the same niche. That is an argument,
not a summary.

**Paragraph two** is the National Careers Service's body — "Highlight the skills and
experience you have that match what the employer is looking for" — and Oxford's "Explain why
you are well-suited to the position". Note the mechanism: it quotes the posting's own phrase
("hold pick accuracy through peak") and answers it with one concrete, dated change he made.
One story argued closely beats five duties restated.

**Paragraph three** follows the National Careers Service's close almost to the letter:
"Thank the employer for considering your application. Let them know that they can get more
details from your CV, and tell them you're looking forward to hearing from them." The
sign-off follows Oxford's rule — "'Yours sincerely' if writing to a named individual and
'Yours faithfully' if you have not been able to find a named contact" — and Ms Achebe is
named, so sincerely it is.

## Why you cannot copy it

Look at which sentences carry the letter:

- "the move into temperature-controlled grocery described in your posting is the operation I
  know from the inside" — only true because this posting announced that move and this
  applicant has that background.
- "Your posting asks for someone who can 'hold pick accuracy through peak'" — quotes this
  advert. Pasted under a different advert, it is instantly, visibly false.
- "how the Doncaster site will handle its first winter peak" — depends on the site being
  new, the sector being seasonal, and him knowing both.

Strip those and what remains is the connective tissue any template already gives you.
**The load-bearing sentences are exactly the unreusable ones — the fit is the content.**
Copying this letter does not get you a good letter minus some details; it gets you a letter
whose every specific claim is wrong. What transfers is the method: one true claim linking
your niche to their situation, one story answering their stated requirement, one close that
points to the CV instead of repeating it.

## Where the institutions differ — and one thing that overrides them all

The convergence is on shape, not detail, and the details genuinely diverge. Harvard gives a
word count — "One page, three to four paragraphs. Aim for 250 to 400 words." The National
Careers Service allows five paragraphs and gives no word count. Oxford measures in paper —
"no longer than 1 side of A4" — and adds a formality the others never mention: "Structure it
like a business letter", with the sincerely/faithfully sign-off rule. A letter following
Harvard's brief could break Oxford's business-letter formality and vice versa; these are
conventions that vary by country and sector, not laws. Check what the target employer and
region expect.

And one instruction outranks all three institutions: the job posting itself. If it says not
to send a cover letter, or to answer set questions instead, that wins — following the
employer's stated process is the whole game.

## What actually costs you

- An opening that restates the CV — the one job all three institutions agree the letter
  should not do.
- A letter that never mentions the employer's name, posting or situation — unanswerable
  "why this employer".
- Reusing one letter across applications with the company name swapped — the specific
  claims stop being true, and it shows.
- Ignoring an instruction in the posting because the "rules" say always send a letter.
- Padding past a page. All three sources cap it there; nobody caps it higher.

## Build it

Write the letter last, after the CV is tailored — the letter argues the fit the CV
