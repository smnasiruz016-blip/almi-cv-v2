We build an AI CV tool, which means we profit every time you believe AI writes great CVs — so
weigh what follows accordingly. AI is genuinely excellent at the mechanics of a CV: structure,
consistency, turning a flat duty into a sharp achievement sentence. It is also entirely capable
of inventing an achievement you never had, in confident, professional prose, and it will never
warn you that it just did.

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents: **treating an AI draft as finished, and sending a CV containing
claims you never made and cannot defend.**

A language model predicts plausible text. Asked to improve "responsible for social media", a
model knows that strong CV bullets contain percentages, so it may produce "grew Instagram
engagement 240% in six months". Plausible. Specific. Fabricated. If you send it, that number is
now *your* claim — and the first interview question about it ends the interview. **A CV is a
signed statement of fact, and you are the one signing it — not the model.**

## What AI reliably does well — our position, from building one

This is opinion from inside the product, not research. Three jobs where we trust the model:

| Job | Why AI is good at it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Formatting consistency | Machines don't get bored: every date in the same format, every bullet starting with a verb, every heading in the same case | Catching "Jan 2022" vs "01/2023" vs "2024–present" in one document |
| Duty → achievement phrasing | Rewriting structure is pattern work — exactly what models do | "Responsible for onboarding" → "Onboarded new starters, cutting time-to-productive work" |
| Tailoring vocabulary | Mapping your wording onto a job advert's wording, without changing the facts | The advert says "stakeholder management"; you wrote "liaised with clients" — same fact, matched vocabulary |

That vocabulary point is legitimate, not gaming: Workday's own explainer says ATS parsing "happens
through natural language processing that recognizes patterns in resume text", understanding that
"'managed team of five' and 'led five-person team' convey similar information". Matching the
advert's terms is for the human skimming a list, as much as for any software.

## Where it fails dangerously

**1. Invented specifics.** The model fills gaps with plausibility. Give it thin input and it will
supply team sizes, percentages, tools and outcomes from nowhere. Watch a real rewrite go wrong:

> **Your input:** "ran the weekly newsletter"
>
> **AI draft:** "Owned weekly newsletter to 12,000 subscribers, lifting open rates from 18% to 31%
> through A/B-tested subject lines."

Walk through it line by line. "Owned weekly newsletter" — true, a fair rephrase of your input.
"12,000 subscribers" — you never said that; the model needed a number and manufactured one.
"18% to 31%" — invented, and dangerously *checkable*: your old employer knows the real figures.
"A/B-tested subject lines" — a method you never mentioned, added because it sounds right. One
true clause, three fabrications, all fluent. The fix is not better AI. The fix is that **you**
supply "8,000 subscribers, open rate up 6 points" and let the model arrange it.

**2. Generic phrasing.** Ask for a CV with no personal facts and every model converges on the
same "results-driven professional with a proven track record" sludge. Here is the anti-template
logic that governs this whole site: **text that any applicant could paste is text that identifies
nobody** — and a CV's only job is to identify you. In Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study, the
initial screen averaged 7.4 seconds — a first skim, not a full read — and interchangeable
phrasing gives that skim nothing to catch on. Your dates, your employers, your numbers are the
un-fake-able part of a CV. No model supplies those; only you do.

## The other side of the table has AI too — in the vendors' own words

Your CV may be drafted with AI and then read by AI. What the hiring-side vendors say their AI
does is narrower than the folklore:

> "Workday AI does not make hiring decisions." — Workday, with "Our AI is designed to
> support—not replace—human decision-making."

Workday's HiredScore is documented as "prioritizing candidates based on comparison of job
requirements and candidates resumes" — prioritisation for humans, who hold the "ability to
advance or reject candidates individually or in bulk". Greenhouse says its Talent Matching "is
designed as assistive AI, not automated decision-making", that it "does not auto-reject or
auto-advance any candidate", and — company position — "We see AI as a co-pilot, not an
auto-pilot." The pattern on both sides of the table is the same: **AI assists; a person decides.
Which is exactly why fabricated specifics are so dangerous — a human will eventually probe them.**

## The honest workflow — ours

This is how we think AI belongs in CV writing, and how the product is built to work:

1. **You supply the facts** — real employers, real dates, real numbers, real tools. Rough notes
   are fine; true is mandatory.
2. **AI drafts** — structure, consistent formatting, duty→achievement phrasing, vocabulary
   matched to the role you are targeting.
3. **You verify every claim** before sending. Read each bullet and ask one question: *did I say
   that, and can I defend it in an interview?* Delete anything you cannot. The model drafted it;
   you are signing it.

## What nobody knows yet

There is, as far as we can find, **no published research on how recruiters receive AI-drafted
CVs** — whether they detect them, whether detection hurts, whether polished-but-generic AI prose
scores worse than rougher human writing. Anyone claiming to know "recruiters reject AI CVs" or
"AI CVs perform better" is guessing. Our advice above is reasoning from how the tools work, not
from outcome data — that data does not exist yet.

## What actually costs you

- A number in your CV that you cannot explain in an interview. Whoever wrote it, you signed it.
- Sending the first AI draft unread. The fluency is exactly what makes fabrication easy to miss.
- "Results-driven professional" padding — text any applicant could paste identifies nobody.
- Giving the model nothing to work with. Thin input does not produce a modest draft; it produces
  an invented one.
- Believing either extreme: "AI writes your CV for you" (our industry's marketing) and "AI CVs
  get auto-rejected" (folklore) are both unsupported.
