The American resume is defined by what it leaves out. Half the world's CVs open with a
photo, a date of birth and a marital status; the US government tells you, in writing, to
include none of it. And the one thing everyone "knew" about American resumes — that federal
ones run long, with no page limit — is no longer true: **USAJOBS now enforces a hard
two-page cap.**

> "Your resume must be two pages or less and explain why you are qualified for the job."
> … "Federal agencies only accept resumes up to two pages in length to comply with the
> Merit Hiring Plan." … "USAJOBS will not allow you to upload or build resumes longer than
> two pages."
> — [USAJOBS](https://help.usajobs.gov/faq/application/documents/resume/what-to-include)

That last sentence is the interesting one: the limit is not advice, it is enforced by the
upload form itself.

## The failure

The failure this page prevents is sending a European-style CV into an American process —
photo top-left, date of birth under your name, "Married, two children" in the personal
details — or, in the other direction, sending a sprawling five-page federal resume built to
a convention that officially died. Both mark the document as written for a different market.

## What stays out — quoted, not implied

The exclusions are explicit in official US guidance, not folklore. USAJOBS, for federal
applications:

> "Do not include the following types of information in your profile or resume: Classified
> or government sensitive information, Social Security Number (SSN), Photos of yourself,
> Personal information, such as age, sex, religious affiliation, etc."
> — [USAJOBS](https://help.usajobs.gov/faq/application/documents/resume/what-to-include)

The universities say the same for the private sector. Harvard's list of don'ts includes
"Include a picture", "Include age or gender", "Use personal pronouns (such as I or We)" and
"List references". MIT: "Don't include personal information about your age, religion,
health or marital status. Photos are generally not preferred for U.S. resumes."

**The design intent is an identity-blind document:** the resume is meant to carry evidence
of what you did, and nothing a screener could use — consciously or not — to judge who you
are. One honest caveat belongs next to that ideal. Bertrand and Mullainathan's 2004 field
experiment (*American Economic Review*) sent nearly 5,000 fictitious resumes to real job
ads with randomly assigned names: "White names receive 50 percent more callbacks for
interviews." Stripping the photo and birth date removes some signals; the study shows a
name alone can still carry one. The convention reduces what the document reveals — it does
not make screening blind.

## "Resume" is the word — "CV" means something else here

In the US the two terms name two different documents. Harvard defines the ordinary one:
"A resume is a concise, informative summary of your abilities, education, and experience."
MIT reserves the other word for academia: "In the United States, curricula vitae (CVs) are
used to apply for teaching or research opportunities, fellowships, further academic
training, grants…" — and gives it a very different length, "typically 2 to 4 pages for a
younger professional, 4 to 7 pages for a person with more experience." MIT adds the warning
that matters for international applicants: "In some international settings, the term 'CV'
is used for all opportunities – however, you should tailor your document based on position
type." The full split is on our [resume vs CV page](/learn/cv-vs-resume).

## The expected shape

Common in the US: reverse-chronological, one to two pages, achievement bullets. CareerOneStop
(US Department of Labor): "Chronological is the most common type of resume. It lists your
work history, with the most recent positions shown first" — and "Employers typically prefer
this type of resume because it's easy to see what jobs you have held and when you worked at
them." Harvard: "Within headings, list information in reverse chronological order (most
recent first)."

On length the American institutions genuinely disagree, and we report rather than resolve
it: MIT says "Stick to one page, unless you have extensive experience or an advanced
degree"; Stanford's handout says "1 – 2 page"; the federal ceiling is two. Worth knowing:
the one-page rule itself is convention, not research — no study supporting it was found,
and the only controlled study on record (ResumeGo, 2018: 482 recruitment professionals,
7,712 resumes, vendor-run and not peer-reviewed) found "recruiters were 2.3 times as likely
to prefer two-page resumes over one-page resumes". The evidence is weighed on our
[length page](/learn/cv-length).

Bullets should be quantified and verb-led — MIT: "Start descriptions with a strong action
verb", "Quantify if you can. If you gave a presentation, include how many people attended."
The full method, with worked rewrites, lives on the bullets page rather than here.

## Federal vs private sector

Federal applications remain more literal than private-sector ones. USAJOBS tells you to
mirror the posting's own terms: "if the qualifications section says you need experience
with MS Project, you need to use the words MS Project in your resume" — and to "tailor your
resume to include experience relevant to each job you want to apply for." The private
sector has no USAJOBS: postings, formats and expectations vary by employer, and none of the
university guidance above is enforced by any upload form.

## What varies, and what the sources don't say

The US is fifty states and no single rulebook. Our sources are federal guidance, the
Department of Labor and elite-university career offices — they say nothing about
state-level differences, nothing sector-by-sector beyond the federal/academic split, and
Stanford's handout is silent on photos and personal data entirely. None of this is law for
a private employer. **Every convention here reads "Common in the US" — check the posting
and the employer's own instructions before you rely on any of it.** One more caution: most
famous resume statistics (six-second skims, ATS rejection rates, 250 applicants per
opening) are US vendor research or pure folklore — treat them accordingly.

## What actually costs you

- A photo, birth date or marital status on an American resume — official guidance bans all three.
- A federal resume over two pages — the USAJOBS builder will not accept it.
- Calling your industry document a "CV" and letting it sprawl like one — in the US that word means the academic document.
- Ignoring the posting's own wording in a federal application — USAJOBS says to use its exact terms.
- First-person pronouns and a references list — both on Harvard's don't list.
