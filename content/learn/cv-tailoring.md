"Tailor your CV" is the one piece of advice every career service agrees on — and almost none of
them explain. It sounds like busywork: rewrite the same document twenty times to flatter twenty
employers. **The real reason is mechanical, and it is documented: the people and systems reading
your CV search it by keyword, and the keywords they search are the posting's own words.**
Tailoring is not reinventing yourself per job. It is making your true experience findable in the
searcher's vocabulary.

## The failure

The failure is describing real, relevant experience in words the searcher never types. You write
"coordinated timelines across workstreams"; the posting — and therefore the recruiter's search
box — says "project management". Your experience matches. Your vocabulary doesn't. The US federal
jobs portal spells out the consequence bluntly enough to quote:

> "Use similar terms and address every required qualification. For example, if the qualifications
> section says you need experience with MS Project, you need to use the words MS Project in your
> resume."
> — USAJOBS

That is a government portal telling applicants, in plain words, to mirror the announcement's
language. It is not a trick. It is how retrieval works.

## Everyone says tailor — here is the chorus

The advice itself is universal across official sources:

> "You should tailor your CV to the job or opportunity you're applying for." … "For example, you
> could highlight skills you have that they have asked for." — UK National Careers Service

> "Target your CV to each position you are applying to, tailoring your content to the job
> description." — Oxford Careers Service

> "Look at the skills required for the position and select experiences where you demonstrated
> those skills." — MIT CAPD

> "Tailor your resume to suit the position you are applying for" — Canada Job Bank

Harvard lists "Failing to tailor the resume to the position or industry" as a top mistake, and
Europass's official CV guidance carries "Tailor your CV" as a heading of its own. The chorus is
real. What the chorus rarely supplies is the mechanism.

## The mechanism, from the vendors' own documentation

Applicant tracking systems turn your CV into a searchable record. Workday's own explainer says
parsing can "Pull critical details from incoming applications without manual data entry, turning
documents into searchable profiles" — and that "Recruiters can also search profiles manually
using filters such as years of experience or required certifications." LinkedIn tells recruiters
its Recruiter product lets them "enter and edit Boolean search strings" across "40+ advanced
search filters". **The searcher's query is built from the job's requirements — which means it is
built from the posting's vocabulary.**

Some systems go further than search. Teamtailor documents automation keyed directly to resume
wording:

> "Automatically move candidates to another stage based on criteria such as answers to questions,
> offer status, keywords in their resume, tags, screening criteria, or location"
> — Teamtailor Support Center ("Triggers"; a Company Admin must activate the feature)

Two honest caveats. Workday also notes that modern systems use language processing that
"understand[s] that 'managed team of five' and 'led five-person team' convey similar information",
and that ranking tools "don't just count matching words" — so exact-match paranoia is overdone.
And no vendor documentation we found says a CV is auto-rejected for missing a keyword. Mirroring
the posting's terms is about being found and recognised, not about passing a secret filter.

## The worked example

**Invented posting excerpt — written for this page, not a real advert:**

> *"The Programme Coordinator will manage stakeholder communications, maintain the project
> budget, and produce monthly progress reports using MS Project."*

The same true experience, written two ways.

**Generic version:**

> Kept various internal and external parties in the loop, looked after spending, and put
> together regular updates on how things were going.

**Mirrored version:**

> Managed stakeholder communications for a 12-partner programme, maintained a £250k project
> budget, and produced monthly progress reports in MS Project.

Per-change reasoning:

- **"Kept various parties in the loop" → "Managed stakeholder communications".** A recruiter
  searching this role types "stakeholder" — the posting's word. "Parties in the loop" is the same
  fact, invisible to that search.
- **"Looked after spending" → "maintained a £250k project budget".** "Budget" is the searchable
  noun the posting uses; the figure adds the scale the posting cannot ask for but every reader
  wants — see the quantified-bullets page.
- **"Regular updates" → "monthly progress reports".** "Progress reports" is the posting's exact
  phrase; "monthly" mirrors its cadence.
- **Naming "MS Project".** The USAJOBS rule verbatim: if the qualifications name a tool, your CV
  needs the tool's name — *only if you actually used it.*

**The honesty rule: mirror vocabulary, never claim skills you lack.** Every change above rewords
a true fact; none invents one. If you never touched MS Project, putting "MS Project" on your CV
is not keyword optimisation — it is lying on a CV, and it fails at the first interview question
about it. Tailoring changes the words, never the truth.

## What not to tailor

Facts stay fixed across every version: dates, job titles, employer names, qualifications, and
numbers. A title on your CV should match the title your former employer would confirm — inflate
it for one application and you have created a discrepancy that a reference check can surface.
Tailor the selection (which experiences you feature, per MIT's advice) and the vocabulary (the
posting's terms) — nothing else.

## What nobody has measured

Here is what the sources do not tell you: **how much tailoring actually moves outcomes.** The
mechanism is documented — recruiters keyword-search, USAJOBS instructs mirroring, Teamtailor
triggers on resume keywords. The effect size is not: we found no credible data on interview or
offer rates for tailored versus untailored CVs, and we will not invent a percentage. Vendors
also differ in framing: iCIMS marketing says candidates "who include more relevant keywords ...
will pass through screening and be ranked higher", while Workday insists its tools "only focus
on the qualifications listed in a candidate's job application" and that "Workday AI does not
make hiring decisions". Both are the vendors' own words; they do not fully agree, and practice
will vary by employer. Treat tailoring as a documented mechanism with an unmeasured payoff —
cheap to do, dishonest to skip describing that way.

## What actually costs you

- Real experience written in vocabulary no searcher would type — right facts, wrong words.
- Naming tools you have never used — keyword-stuffing false claims is lying, and it surfaces.
- Tailoring titles, dates, or numbers instead of vocabulary and selection.
- Ignoring a named qualification the posting states — USAJOBS: "address every required
  qualification".
- One generic CV for every application, when every official source from Oxford to Canada's Job
  Bank says tailor.
