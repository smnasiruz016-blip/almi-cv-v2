You do not need a career coach to settle the LinkedIn-versus-CV question — LinkedIn has already
settled it in writing. Its own guidance says flatly:

> "your LinkedIn profile isn't a résumé—it's your online reputation."

**One is a tailored document you send to one employer; the other is a standing public record
that recruiters search.** Everything else about how to write each one follows from that single
difference.

## The failure

The failure is symmetry: treating the two as one text in two places. Paste your CV into your
profile and you get a page LinkedIn's own talent guidance warns against —

> "A generic profile that reads like a resume won't stand out"

— and write your CV like a profile bio and you get a document with no target: first-person
storytelling, no tailoring to the job in front of it, the same file sent everywhere. Each format
then fails at the other's job. The profile is never *sent* to anyone, so tailoring it to one
vacancy is wasted effort; the CV is never *searched* by strangers, so writing it as a general
reputation piece wastes the one chance to answer a specific advert.

## What LinkedIn says each one is for

The "isn't a résumé" line comes from a LinkedIn guide on building an effective profile; the
company's About page describes the platform it sits on — "more than 1 billion members in more
than 200 countries and territories worldwide", with a mission to "connect the world's
professionals to make them more productive and successful". A profile is your entry in that
public directory. LinkedIn's advice for it is reputation advice, not document advice: on the
headline, "It's the single-most-important factor to make a strong first impression"; on social
proof, "Recommendations from previous hires, colleagues, and clients is one of the most viewed
areas of a LinkedIn profile". Its completeness figures — from the same page — are engagement
claims about the platform: "21x more profile views with a picture", a "9x increase in connection
requests", "36x more likely to get messages". These are LinkedIn's numbers about LinkedIn; we
quote them as the vendor's own marketing, not as independent research.

Nowhere in that guidance is anything about tailoring to a vacancy — because a profile has no
single reader. A CV does.

## The search reality

Here is the mechanism that makes the two documents different. LinkedIn Recruiter — the paid
product recruiters use — is a search engine over profiles. LinkedIn's feature page:

> "Filter by candidate requirements like job titles, skills, locations, and more using 40+
> advanced filters"

> "Narrow or expand your keyword searches to find the exact profiles you're seeking"

Its recruiter-training material adds that recruiters "enter and edit Boolean search strings.
This allows you to retrieve data from an entire profile page", and that Recruiter can "Identify
'Open to work' talent instantly".

**The profile is found; the CV is sent.** A recruiter running a filtered search never sees your
CV — they see whatever is in your profile's structured fields: job titles, skills, locations.
A hiring manager reading your application never runs a search over it — they read the document
you chose to send. Two different retrieval mechanisms, two different writing problems.

| | LinkedIn profile | CV |
|---|---|---|
| Reader arrives by | searching ("40+ advanced filters", Boolean) | receiving what you sent |
| Audience | anyone, indefinitely | one employer, one vacancy |
| Versions | one, standing | one per application |
| What matters | findability: titles, skills, completeness | fit: tailored evidence for this role |

## What that implies for each

**Our coaching — the following is inference from LinkedIn's published mechanics, not a LinkedIn
quote.**

- *On the profile, completeness and searchable terms matter.* If recruiters filter by "job
  titles, skills, locations", then empty skills fields and vague titles make you unfindable —
  not rejected, simply never retrieved. Fill the structured fields; use the job titles your
  target market actually searches for.
- *On the CV, tailoring matters.* The CV is read by someone who already has a vacancy in mind.
  Generic text that would be fine on a standing public page reads as effort not made. See the
  tailoring page for the discipline.
- *The two must agree on facts.* Your profile is public and standing; your CV is in the same
  recruiter's other hand. Dates, titles and employers that differ between them are a
  discoverable inconsistency — anyone can compare the document you sent with the page anyone
  can look up. Keep one canonical set of dates and titles, and let the CV differ only in
  emphasis and selection, never in fact.

## What we could not check

Honesty about the edges of this page's sources. Both quotes framing the profile-versus-résumé
distinction come from LinkedIn's *business* pages — one written for a sales audience, one for a
recruiting audience. LinkedIn's consumer Help Centre (linkedin.com/help) blocks automated
fetching, so **no consumer-facing LinkedIn statement on profile versus CV could be verified** —
including the wording of the Career Breaks profile feature, which would be directly relevant to
readers with employment gaps. If that wording matters to you, read it on LinkedIn directly.
We also found no data on how many recruiters actually cross-check a profile against a submitted
CV — the consistency advice above rests on the fact that they *can*, not on a measured rate.

## What actually costs you

- A profile that is a pasted CV — LinkedIn's own guidance: it "won't stand out".
- Empty skills and title fields — recruiters filter on exactly those; you are simply not in the
  result set.
- A CV written like a bio — untargeted text sent to a reader with a specific vacancy.
- Dates or titles that disagree between profile and CV — a public, checkable inconsistency.
- Tailoring the profile to one job — the profile has every future reader; the CV has this one.

## Build it

Keep the profile complete and searchable; keep the CV tailored and sendable; keep the facts
identical across both. The CV half of that starts with a clean, parseable document.
