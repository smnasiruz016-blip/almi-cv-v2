Ask Oxford's career service what a CV is and you get one answer. Ask MIT's and you get a
different document entirely — different length, different purpose, different audience.
**Whether "CV" and "resume" mean the same thing depends entirely on where you are standing,
and both career services are right for their own market.**

Here is Oxford, defining the ordinary job document every UK applicant sends:

> "A curriculum vitae (or résumé in the US) is a concise summary of your skills,
> achievements, and interests inside and outside of your academic work."
> — [Oxford University Careers Service](https://www.careers.ox.ac.uk/cvs)

And here is MIT, defining something else altogether:

> "In the United States, curricula vitae (CVs) are used to apply for teaching or research
> opportunities, fellowships, further academic training, grants, contract funding proposals,
> tenure, promotion to professor positions, and membership in a professional society or
> organization."
> — [MIT Career Advising & Professional Development](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/cvs/)

Same two letters. Two institutions, two definitions, zero contradiction — because there are
really three documents hiding behind two words.

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents is sending the wrong document because you translated the
word instead of the request. A UK applicant hears a US employer ask for a "resume", panics,
and strips their perfectly normal two-page CV down to something skeletal it never needed to
be. Worse: a US PhD applying for an industry job attaches their 6-page academic CV because
the London posting said "CV" — burying the relevant experience under conference
presentations the reader never asked for. Harvard's PhD guidance describes exactly the
document that posting actually wanted:

> "A resume is typically shorter, 1-2 pages at most, and will dedicate more space to your
> experience while focusing less on academic awards, conference presentations, and
> publications."
> — [Harvard Mignone Center](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/resumes-cover-letters-for-phd-students/)

**The word on the posting is a regional label, not a specification. The specification is
the role.**

## The three documents

**1. The UK/Commonwealth CV — the normal job document.** This is what "CV" means in
Britain, Ireland, and much of the Commonwealth: the standard 1–2 page application document.
The UK's official careers service defines it with no academic flavour at all:

> "A CV is a summary of your skills, achievements and experience that you use to apply for
> jobs."
> — [UK National Careers Service](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections)

Oxford's advice on length matches: "Keep it to one or two full pages (academic CVs may be
longer)". In this market, "résumé" is simply the American name for the same thing — Oxford
says so in its opening sentence.

**2. The US resume — the normal job document, under its American name.** Harvard's
definition is almost interchangeable with the UK government's definition of a CV:

> "A resume is a concise, informative summary of your abilities, education, and experience."
> — [Harvard Mignone Center](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/)

Functionally, document 1 and document 2 are the same artefact wearing different name tags.
The genuinely different one is the third.

**3. The US/academic CV — the long scholarly record.** This is MIT's document: the
comprehensive record of an academic career, used for the purposes MIT lists above —
teaching and research posts, fellowships, grants, tenure. It is not a compressed summary
and is not meant to be short:

> "The length is typically 2 to 4 pages for a younger professional, 4 to 7 pages for a
> person with more experience."
> — [MIT Career Advising & Professional Development](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/cvs/)

A 6-page document is a failure as a resume and completely normal as an academic CV. Length
is not a quality signal here — it is a genre marker.

## Which one to send

| Where you are applying | Kind of role | Send this | Locals call it |
|---|---|---|---|
| UK / Ireland / Commonwealth | Ordinary industry/public-sector job | 1–2 page summary document | "CV" |
| US / Canada | Ordinary industry job | 1–2 page summary document | "resume" |
| Anywhere | Faculty post, research post, fellowship, grant, tenure case | Full scholarly record, 2–7 pages | "CV" (academic CV) |
| US, academic → industry move | Industry job | 1–2 page summary — convert, don't attach the academic CV | "resume" |

The first two rows are the same document. Only the third row is a genuinely different
genre — and MIT flags the trap for people crossing between markets:

> "In some international settings, the term 'CV' is used for all opportunities – however,
> you should tailor your document based on position type."
> — [MIT Career Advising & Professional Development](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/cvs/)

That sentence is the whole page in miniature: the label follows geography, the document
follows the position.

## What is genuinely unsettled

Usage varies, and no institution owns the words. Oxford and MIT are quoted above making
formally incompatible statements — Oxford treats CV and résumé as one document with two
names; MIT treats them as two documents — and neither is wrong, because each describes its
own market. We are not going to resolve that for them, and you should not try either.

What settles it in practice is the employer's own wording, read for intent rather than
vocabulary. A London posting that says "resume" almost certainly wants the ordinary 1–2
page document — the standard UK CV — not an American import or an academic record. A
Boston posting that says "CV" for a lab or faculty role wants the long MIT-style document;
a Boston startup that says "CV" (perhaps written by a European founder) almost certainly
wants a resume. **When the posting's wording and your regional habit conflict, the posting
wins — and if you genuinely cannot tell, check with the employer.** Common in the UK: "CV"
for everything. Common in the US: "resume" for industry, "CV" for academia. Neither
convention is a rule anywhere else.

## What actually costs you

- **Sending an academic CV to an industry employer.** The relevant experience Harvard says
  a resume should foreground gets buried under publications the reader will not use.
- **Padding a 1-page US resume into 4 pages because a European posting said "CV".** The
  UK/Commonwealth CV is 1–2 pages too — Oxford's words, "one or two full pages".
- **Amputating a genuine academic CV to fit an imagined 1-page rule.** For fellowships,
  grants and faculty posts, MIT's stated range is 2–7 pages; the record is the point.
- **Translating the word instead of reading the role.** Geography sets the label; the
  position type sets the document.
