Institutional career guides agree on almost nothing. One page or two — split. References on the
CV — the UK government says yes, Harvard and Canada say no. Photos — depends on the country. But
put Harvard, MIT, Oxford, Stanford, the Canadian government and the US federal jobs portal side by
side and one sentence survives in every voice: **describe what you accomplished, not what you were
responsible for — and put a number on it.**

Here is that sentence, four ways:

> "Fact-based (quantify and qualify)" — how resume language should read, per **Harvard's**
> Mignone Center, which also lists "Using passive language instead of 'action' words" among its
> top five mistakes.

> "Quantify if you can. If you gave a presentation, include how many people attended." — **MIT**

> "CONTEXT: Setting…, ACCOMPLISHMENTS: Overall task/project/goal accomplished…, RESULTS: Outcomes
> of actions (quantify results when possible)" — **Stanford's** C-A-R accomplishment formula

> "Use firm numbers that the employer will understand and be impressed by. For example, how many
> people you supervised, how many products you sold, by what percentage you increased sales, etc."
> — **Canada Job Bank**

Oxford says the same for the UK: use "numbers, percentages, and values to quantify your impact and
give a sense of scale/context". (One region note: UK sources call this document a CV, US sources a
resume — the bullet advice is identical on both sides.)

## The failure

The failure is the duty bullet — a line that paraphrases your job description instead of your
performance. "Responsible for opening the store." "Handled customer complaints." "Trained new
staff." Every other person who ever held the job could paste in the same lines, because the lines
describe the job, not you.

Duty bullets fail twice. A reader learns nothing that the job title didn't already say. And they
tend to arrive in the passive, noun-heavy phrasing Harvard flags — "responsible for", "duties
included" — instead of the action verbs MIT ("built, managed, developed, wrote") and Canada Job
Bank ("handled, managed, led, developed, increased") tell you to open with.

**A duty says what the job was. An achievement says what happened because you did it — and the
proof is a number and a consequence.**

## The teardown: four bullets, rewritten

An invented example — a shift supervisor at a small grocery chain. The facts are made up; the
method is not. Your rewrites must use your real numbers.

**1. Before:** *Responsible for opening and closing the store.*
**After:** *Opened and closed a 12-staff store four days a week, with no missed opening in two
years.*
What changed: "responsible for" became a verb, and the store got a size. Why the number matters:
"12-staff" tells a reader the scale of what you held keys to. Where the consequence lives: "no
missed opening" — reliability, stated as a track record rather than claimed as a trait.

**2. Before:** *Handled customer complaints.*
**After:** *Resolved 15–20 customer complaints a week at the till, escalating roughly one a month
to the store manager.*
What changed: "handled" became "resolved" — an outcome verb. Why the numbers matter: 15–20 a week
is volume; one escalation a month against that volume is the consequence — nearly everything ended
with you. A range is fine; nobody counted, and a range reads more honest than false precision.

**3. Before:** *Trained new staff.*
**After:** *Trained nine new checkout staff over two years; each ran a till unsupervised within
two weeks.*
What changed: this is Stanford's C-A-R in one line — context (two years, checkout), accomplishment
(trained nine), result (independent within two weeks). Why it matters: "trained new staff" could
mean one afternoon with one hire. The consequence is the second clause: your training produced
people who no longer needed you.

**4. Before:** *Managed stock and reduced waste.*
**After:** *Cut fresh-produce write-offs by around 15% over six months by moving to twice-daily
stock rotation.*
What changed: the vague pair "managed and reduced" became one mechanism and one measured result.
Why it matters: this is exactly Canada Job Bank's "by what percentage" pattern — a percentage
travels across industries; a reader who has never run a grocery aisle still understands 15% less
waste. The consequence is the number itself; the mechanism ("twice-daily rotation") is what makes
it credible.

Notice what every rewrite shares: a verb up front, a number in the middle, a consequence at the
end. That is the whole trick.

## When the numbers honestly don't exist

Plenty of jobs never hand you a percentage — carers, receptionists, most first jobs. The
institutions already allow for this: MIT says quantify *"if you can"*, Stanford says *"when
possible"*. Neither demands a metric on every line, and none of them licenses inventing one.

What follows is our coaching, not institutional guidance: when there is no result to measure,
quantify the *shape* of the work instead —

- **Scope:** how many people, tills, sites, patients, accounts the work touched.
- **Frequency:** per shift, per week, per term — "answered a 40-call day" beats "answered phones".
- **Comparison:** against the before-state or the standard — "first in the team trained on the new
  system", "covered two roles during a six-month vacancy".

An honest estimate ("roughly", "around", a range) is fine. A number you could not defend in an
interview is not — the bullet gets you a conversation, and the conversation checks the bullet.

One more reason the rewritten versions work harder, from the software side: Workday's own ATS
explainer describes parsing as "turning documents into searchable profiles", with recruiters
searching them manually by filters and terms — **so the concrete nouns in your bullets (till,
stock rotation, escalation, the systems you name) are findable content, not decoration.** A duty
bullet has fewer of them by construction.

## What actually costs you

- Bullets that restate the job advert — any holder of the title could have written them.
- "Responsible for" and "duties included" openings where a verb should be.
- Vague intensifiers ("significantly improved") standing where a number could stand.
- Numbers without consequences — "processed 200 items an hour" only lands if the reader can tell
  whether that is a lot.
- Invented precision. A defensible range beats an indefensible decimal.
