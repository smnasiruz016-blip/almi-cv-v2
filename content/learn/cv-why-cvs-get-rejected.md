"The ATS rejected me" is a comforting story — it means the failure was a robot's, not yours,
and the fix is a trick, not work. The documented reality is less dramatic and more useful:
rejection has four researched sources, and formatting-reading robots are barely one of them.
**Each of the four has real evidence behind it — and each piece of evidence has limits worth
stating out loud.**

## The failure

The mistake this page prevents is misdiagnosis. If you believe a parser binned you for using
the wrong font, you will spend your energy on "ATS-beating" templates and keyword stuffing —
and none of it touches the things that actually filter people out. Fix the diagnosis first,
then spend effort where it can matter.

## The four documented sources of rejection

| # | Source of rejection | Evidence type | Grade of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Knockout / eligibility answers | ATS vendors' own documentation | Strong — it is a shipped, documented feature |
| 2 | Employer-configured filter criteria | Harvard/Accenture surveys (8,000+ workers, 2,250+ executives) | Good — but self-reported by employers |
| 3 | Human judgment, fast | Vendor docs + Enhancv interviews (25 recruiters) + Ladders eye-tracking | Mixed — consistent direction, small samples |
| 4 | Discrimination | Peer-reviewed field experiment (~5,000 CVs) | Gold standard — audit design, replicated |

### 1. You answered a knockout question the wrong way

This is the one genuine auto-reject, and the vendors document it themselves. Greenhouse's
support pages describe the feature plainly:

> "based on an applicant's answer to a question, they will automatically be rejected as a
> potential candidate"

Oracle Taleo's documentation says a candidate failing a disqualification question "can be
instantly exited from the application process". These rules run on structured application
questions — work authorisation, licences, location — configured by the employer.
**No vendor documentation found in our research says an ATS rejects a CV for its formatting,
fonts, columns or missing keywords.** When parsing fails at Greenhouse, the documented
consequence is that a recruiter types your details in manually — not rejection.

### 2. The employer's filter criteria screened you out

This is the legitimate core of the "software hides candidates" story — and it is
human-configured, not robotic. Harvard Business School and Accenture's *Hidden Workers*
research (2021) surveyed over 8,000 workers and 2,250 executives in the US, UK and Germany:

> "A large majority (88 per cent) of employers agree, telling us that qualified high-skills
> candidates are vetted out of the process because they do not match the exact criteria
> established by the job description. That number rose to 94 per cent in the case of
> middle-skills workers."

Read that carefully: employers themselves admit their filters exclude qualified people. But
the filters are criteria people chose — degree requirements, employment-gap screens, exact
job-description matching — not a parser objecting to your template. The same report found
more than 90 per cent of employers use their systems to filter or rank candidates. It never
says "75%", and it is about ranking rules, not formatting.

### 3. A human decided, quickly

Most rejection is a person saying no. Enhancv's 2025 study interviewed 25 US recruiters —
a small sample, stated openly — and found:

> "23 of 25 (92%) said rejections are manual or triggered only by eligibility filters —
> never by formatting or missing keywords."

The vendors say the same about their own AI. Workday: "Workday AI does not make hiring
decisions." Greenhouse: "humans decide how to advance, reject, or follow up with each
candidate." The human decision is fast, though: Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study put "the
average initial screen clocking in at just 7.4 seconds" — an initial fit/no-fit skim, not a
full read, from a small non-peer-reviewed vendor study (the 2012 version used about 30
recruiters). Treat the number as directional: **the first pass on your CV is a skim, and
skims reward pages whose top half carries the load.**

### 4. Discrimination

Pretending this source does not exist would be dishonest. Bertrand and Mullainathan's field
experiment (*American Economic Review*, 2004) sent nearly 5,000 fictitious CVs to over 1,300
job ads, randomly assigning names:

> "White names receive 50 percent more callbacks for interviews... The racial gap is uniform
> across occupation, industry, and employer size."

This is the gold-standard audit design, and a large 2022 replication (Kline, Rose & Walters,
83,000+ applications to Fortune 500 firms) found contact gaps concentrated in a subset of
companies. It is the best-evidenced item on this page — and the one no template can fix.

## What you can influence — and what you cannot

- **Knockouts (1): fully in your hands.** Read the application questions as carefully as the
  job advert; they are the only true auto-reject. Answer honestly — a knockout passed by
  lying fails later.
- **Filter criteria (2): partially.** You cannot change an employer's degree screen, but you
  can tailor your CV to the stated criteria and address gaps directly rather than hoping
  they go unnoticed.
- **The fast human read (3): substantially.** Coaching, plainly labelled as coaching: put
  your strongest, most specific evidence — quantified bullets, current title, relevant
  scope — where a skim lands first. Parseable and specific beats clever.
- **Discrimination (4): not yours to fix.** No CV advice resolves it, and we will not
  pretend otherwise. A rejection is weak evidence about your CV at the best of times; here
  it may be no evidence at all.

## What nobody actually knows

No employer or ATS vendor publishes real rejection-reason data — how many applicants fell
at each of the four gates. Everything above is the best evidence that exists, with its
limits: vendor documentation describes features, not frequencies; the Harvard figures are
employers' self-reports; Enhancv interviewed 25 recruiters; the eye-tracking studies were
job-board marketing research; Bertrand and Mullainathan is US data from 2004 newspaper ads.
The honest position is that the *mix* of the four is unmeasured — but the direction of every
source available points the same way: away from formatting robots, towards eligibility
rules, human criteria and human judgment.

## What actually costs you

- Failing a knockout question you skim-read — the one documented auto-reject.
- Not matching the criteria the job description actually states.
- A top half that gives a fast human reader nothing specific to hold on to.
- Blaming the parser, then spending your effort on tricks instead of evidence.
- Treating any single rejection as a verdict on your CV — the data cannot support that.
