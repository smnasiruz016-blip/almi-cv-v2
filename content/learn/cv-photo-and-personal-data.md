A CV header with a photo, a date of birth and "Married, two children" is not universally wrong.
It is wrong *somewhere specific* — and right somewhere else. **Anglophone official guidance is
unusually blunt about leaving personal data off; other markets treat some of the same details as
customary.** The document does not change; the border does.

## The failure

The failure is carrying one market's convention across a border without noticing. A candidate who
learned to write applications where a photo and birthdate are standard sends that same header to a
London or Toronto employer, where every official source says to cut it — and where, per Canada's
Job Bank, a photo "can actually lower your chances". The reverse failure exists too: stripping a
CV to anglophone austerity, then wondering why it looks oddly bare to an employer whose local
convention expects more. Neither candidate is careless. Both are following a rule that stopped at
a border they did not know was there.

## What UK, US and Canadian official sources say — verbatim

The consistency across these three countries' official guidance is striking. These are government
employment services and university careers offices, not blog folklore:

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| UK National Careers Service | "You should not include your age, your date of birth, whether you're married or your nationality." |
| Oxford Careers Service | "You don't need to include the following in a CV: … Date of birth and/or age… Marital status, disability, children, partner, sexual preferences, sex, racial background, religion… Home address." |
| Canada Job Bank | A photo "is not the norm in Canada. It can actually lower your chances" — and "leave out any personal details such as age, weight, height, marital status", and "never include your Social Insurance Number in your resume". |
| USAJOBS (US federal) | "Do not include the following types of information in your profile or resume: … Social Security Number (SSN), Photos of yourself, Personal information, such as age, sex, religious affiliation, etc." |
| Harvard | Among the don'ts: "Include a picture" and "Include age or gender". |
| MIT | "Don't include personal information about your age, religion, health or marital status. Photos are generally not preferred for U.S. resumes." |

Read the wording closely, because the sources themselves admit the border. Canada's Job Bank does
not say a photo is wrong — it says it "is not the norm *in Canada*". MIT says photos "are
generally not preferred *for U.S. resumes*". **Official guidance in these countries is scoped to
these countries, and the institutions writing it know it.**

## A worked teardown

Here is the header the guidance above is aimed at:

> **Anna Novak** · [photo] · Born 14.03.1994 · Married, no children · Austrian citizen ·
> anna.novak@example.com · +44 7…

Line by line, for a UK, US or Canadian application:

- **Photo** — cut. Job Bank: "not the norm in Canada. It can actually lower your chances."
  USAJOBS bans it outright for federal resumes; Harvard lists it as a don't.
- **Date of birth** — cut. The UK National Careers Service names age and date of birth first
  among the things you "should not include".
- **Marital status** — cut. Named explicitly by the National Careers Service, Oxford, Job Bank
  and MIT.
- **Nationality** — cut for the UK: the National Careers Service says not to include it. (If a
  posting asks about your right to work, answer that question where it is asked — the
  application form — not in your CV header.)
- **Name, email, phone** — keep. That is the whole header these sources want.

Five items become three, and nothing an employer legitimately needs has been lost.

## Why anglophone guidance is like this

The logic is anti-discrimination: age, marital status, appearance and origin are characteristics
employers in these jurisdictions are not supposed to weigh, so the convention is to keep them off
the page entirely — protecting the candidate and the employer at once.

There is hard research behind taking that logic seriously. Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil
Mullainathan ran the field experiment (*American Economic Review*, 2004), sending nearly 5,000
fictitious resumes to over 1,300 help-wanted ads in Boston and Chicago, randomly assigning
"African-American- or White-sounding names" to otherwise comparable resumes. From the abstract:

> "White names receive 50 percent more callbacks for interviews. Callbacks are also more
> responsive to resume quality for White names than for African-American ones. The racial gap is
> uniform across occupation, industry, and employer size."

Note what this does and does not show. It demonstrates that identity signals on a resume — here,
just a name — measurably shift screening outcomes; that is the research reality behind
identity-blind conventions. It does not show that removing a photo or birthdate guarantees fair
treatment, and a name cannot be removed at all. **The convention reduces the surface for bias; it
does not eliminate it.**

## Convention, not rule — and the two-version answer

Outside the UK, US and Canada, the same details can be customary rather than suspect. Our
country-specific convention pages carry the per-market detail; the principle here is that none of
this is law-of-nature — it is local hiring custom, and official sources only ever speak for their
own market.

The practical answer when applying across borders: **make two versions.** One identity-light
version — name and contact details only in the header — for UK, US and Canadian applications; one
version matching the target market's local convention for markets that expect more. Maintaining
two headers costs minutes. Sending the wrong one costs the first impression.

## What is not settled

- **Conventions shift over time.** The guidance quoted here is what these institutions publish
  now; markets that expect photos today may not in ten years, and vice versa.
- **We could not verify Australian government wording** on photos, age and marital status — its
  official pages resist automated checking — so we make no claim about Australia here.
- **Stanford's handout is silent** on personal data; absence of advice is not advice.
- **The target employer's posting outranks every guide, including this one.** If an application
  explicitly requests a photo or a birthdate, the request wins — that is house policy on every
  page of this site.

## What actually costs you

- Sending a photo-and-birthdate header to a UK, US or Canadian employer — Job Bank says the
  photo alone "can actually lower your chances".
- Putting an SSN or SIN on any resume, anywhere. USAJOBS and Job Bank both prohibit it flatly.
- Stripping a CV to anglophone convention for a market that expects more, without checking that
  market's own guidance.
- Treating any of this as universal. Every source quoted above scopes itself to its own country.
